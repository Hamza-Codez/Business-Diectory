Ask how a business gets discovered online and you'll hear about advertising, social media, influencers. But underneath all of it sits something far less glamorous that decides whether a ready-to-buy customer actually reaches you: your basic information, correct and consistent, everywhere it appears.

## NAP: the three facts that do the work

Name, address, phone — NAP, in local-search jargon. Directories, map applications, and search engines cross-reference these three facts across the web, and consistency is the trust signal. The same name spelled the same way, the same address formatted the same way, the same phone number — everywhere.

The most common self-inflicted wound is drift: 田中商店 on one platform, "Tanaka Shoten Co." on another, an old address on a third. Each variation splinters your identity into what machines treat as possibly-different businesses. Pick one canonical version of each fact — including one romanization if you serve non-Japanese customers — write it in a file, and paste from that file forever.

## Hours are a promise

An online listing that says you're open when you're closed doesn't just lose that customer; it teaches them your information can't be trusted. Post your true hours including the last order time if you're a restaurant, your teikyūbi closing day, and — the step most businesses skip — your holiday closures. Ten minutes updating your listings before New Year, Golden Week, and Obon puts you ahead of most competitors, because "are they open during the holiday?" is precisely when customers check listings hardest.

## The open-data multiplier

Here's the piece few business owners know. A large share of the world's map and directory services — including this one — draw from OpenStreetMap, an openly licensed map database maintained like Wikipedia. When your business is present and accurate in OpenStreetMap — name, position, address, phone, opening hours — that correct data propagates outward into navigation apps, delivery platforms, research tools, and directories built on the same open data.

Anyone can add or fix a business on openstreetmap.org; the edit takes minutes and becomes part of shared public infrastructure. One accurate entry there quietly improves your presence in places you've never heard of. It's the closest thing local online visibility has to compound interest.

## Photos and the honesty rule

Real photographs outperform polished ones. Your actual entrance (so customers recognize it from the street), your actual interior, your actual products or work. The entrance photo is the most underrated: in a country of unnamed streets, "does this match what I'm looking at?" is often the final step of every customer's journey to your door.

*One canonical file with your exact name, address, phone, and hours — every listing gets pasted from it, and drift never starts.*

## Answer the phone, answer the form

Listings generate contact attempts, and in Japan a promptly answered phone remains a powerful trust signal — many customers, particularly older ones, will call before they visit and won't leave a message. If staffing the phone is hard, state your reachable hours honestly on your listings. For inquiry forms and email, same-day or next-morning replies are the local standard; a fast, courteous reply to a small question is frequently what converts it into a visit.

## The one-hour audit

Sit down once a season and check: is the NAP identical everywhere your business appears? Are the hours current, including upcoming holidays? Is the entrance photo recent? Does the phone number ring? That single hour, four times a year, is the highest-return marketing most small businesses will ever do — because everything louder you build on top of it depends on these plain facts being right.
