Every listing in a directory ultimately comes down to one thing: can you find the place? In Japan, that depends on understanding an address system that works in the opposite direction from most of the world — and once you see the logic, it never confuses you again.

## Largest to smallest

A Japanese address zooms in like a camera. It starts with the prefecture, narrows to the city or ward, then to a district, and finally to a numbered block and building. Written in Japanese, a Tokyo address reads: 東京都渋谷区神南1丁目2番3号 — Tokyo Metropolis, Shibuya Ward, Jinnan district, 1-chōme, block 2, building 3.

When the same address is romanized for international use, the order flips to match Western convention: 1-2-3 Jinnan, Shibuya-ku, Tokyo. Same place, mirrored order. This flip is the single biggest source of confusion — if an address looks "backwards," it's usually just been converted between the two conventions.

## The three numbers

That trio of numbers — 1-2-3 in our example — is the heart of the system. The first is the chōme, a numbered neighborhood subdivision. The second is the banchi, a city block within that chōme. The third is the gō, the individual building on that block.

Notice what's missing: a street name. With few exceptions (Kyoto's grid being the famous one), Japanese streets are unnamed. Blocks carry the identity instead. Buildings on a block are typically numbered in the order they were registered, not in walking order — which is why even locals check a map for the final few meters.

## The postal code does heavy lifting

Every address begins with 〒 and a seven-digit postal code, written NNN-NNNN. Japanese postal codes are precise — often resolving to a single district or even a large building — so pasting the postal code into any map application gets you most of the way before you even parse the rest. When a listing gives you both a postal code and the number trio, you have everything you need.

## Prefectures come in four flavors

Japan's 47 prefectures aren't all labeled the same way. Most end in -ken (県), like Kanagawa-ken. Tokyo is a to (都), Hokkaido is a dō (道), and Osaka and Kyoto are fu (府). They all function the same way for addressing purposes; the suffix is history, not hierarchy.

Inside large cities you'll see -ku (区) for wards, -shi (市) for cities, and in rural areas -gun (郡) for districts containing towns (-chō/-machi) and villages (-mura/-son).

## Finding the door in practice

A reliable routine for visiting an unfamiliar business: put the postal code into your map app and confirm the district; match the chōme and block from the listing; then, on arrival, look for the blue-and-white address plates mounted on buildings and utility poles. If you're lost within a block, a nearby kōban — the small neighborhood police box — exists partly for exactly this; giving directions is a normal part of the job.

Business listings often add a practical extra line: the nearest station and walking time, such as "3 minutes from Shibuya Station, Hachikō exit." In everyday Japanese life, that line is used more than the formal address itself. When a listing shows both, trust the station line for planning your trip and the formal address for the final approach.

Once the largest-to-smallest logic clicks, Japanese addresses turn out to be remarkably rational — a system built around blocks and precision codes instead of street names. The address on any listing isn't a puzzle; it's a set of coordinates in disguise.
