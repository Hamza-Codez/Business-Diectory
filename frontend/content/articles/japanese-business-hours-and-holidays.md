The hours line on a listing — something like `Mo-Fr 09:00-18:00; Sa 09:00-13:00; Su, PH off` — is short, but reading it well requires knowing the rhythms behind it. Japanese business hours follow patterns that surprise first-timers, and a few calendar clusters can close entire industries for days.

## Typical hours by type of business

Offices generally run 9:00 to 18:00 on weekdays. Banks are the famous outlier: branch counters typically operate 9:00 to 15:00, weekdays only, though ATMs run much longer. Post offices keep bank-like counter hours for mail, with larger branches open later and on weekends.

Retail opens late by Western standards — department stores and malls commonly at 10:00 or 11:00 — and closes around 20:00. Convenience stores are the 24-hour exception that proves the rule. Clinics frequently split their day into morning and afternoon consultation blocks with a long midday break, and many close one weekday plus Sunday.

## The restaurant split and the last order

Many restaurants serve lunch from about 11:30 to 14:00, close entirely through the afternoon, and reopen for dinner from 17:00 or 18:00. Arriving at 15:30 expecting a meal is a classic mistake.

Just as important is the last order (ラストオーダー). A restaurant listing hours until 22:00 usually stops taking orders 30 to 60 minutes earlier. For a proper meal, aim to arrive at least an hour before closing.

## Teikyūbi: the regular closing day

Smaller businesses — restaurants, salons, independent shops, clinics — commonly take a fixed weekly closing day called teikyūbi (定休日). Monday and Wednesday are popular choices, and museums famously close on Mondays. This is why a listing's hours string matters more than an "open daily" assumption: the day that's missing from the string is the teikyūbi.

## The three great closures

Three periods a year reshape the entire calendar, and any visit or business dealing should be planned around them:

**New Year (roughly December 29 – January 3).** The most complete shutdown of the year. Offices, banks, small shops, and many restaurants close; January 1 is a national holiday and the days around it are customary closures.

**Golden Week (April 29 – May 5).** A cluster of four national holidays — Shōwa Day, Constitution Memorial Day, Greenery Day, and Children's Day — that most offices bridge into a full week off. Tourist businesses boom; corporate Japan pauses.

**Obon (around August 13–16).** Not an official national holiday, but a deep-rooted tradition of returning to family hometowns. Many companies and small businesses close for several days, and transport is at its most crowded.

## Reading the PH in a listing

National holidays are marked PH (public holiday) in standardized hours strings. `Su, PH off` means Sundays and all national holidays. Japan has more than a dozen national holidays spread through the year, and when one falls on a Sunday, the following Monday becomes a substitute holiday — so a Monday closure can occasionally surprise you even at a business that's normally open.

## The practical routine

Before visiting any smaller business: check the hours on its listing, note which weekday is missing, and if your visit falls anywhere near late December, late April, or mid-August, confirm by phone. A thirty-second call — or noticing whether the noren is out — saves an hour's trip. The hours are rarely wrong; the calendar context around them is what catches people.
