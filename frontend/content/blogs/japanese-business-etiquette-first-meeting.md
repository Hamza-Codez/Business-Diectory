You've found the company in the directory, made contact, and secured a meeting. Now the part that makes many visitors nervous: the etiquette. Here's the honest version — a handful of moments genuinely matter, sincerity covers most gaps, and nobody expects a foreign visitor to perform perfectly.

## Arrive early, not on time

Punctuality in Japan means arriving before the appointed time. Ten minutes early is professional; exactly on time reads as cutting it close; late — even five minutes — requires a phone call ahead and an apology on arrival. If you plan around trains, this is easy: they're punctual too.

## The meishi exchange

The business card (meishi) exchange opens nearly every first meeting, and it's the ritual most worth learning properly.

Offer your card with both hands, print facing the recipient so they can read it, stating your company and name. Receive theirs with both hands as well, and — this is the part people forget — actually read it for a moment. The card stands in for the person; glancing at it and pocketing it immediately is the mistake to avoid. During the meeting, place received cards on the table in front of you, arranged to match the seating. Put them away respectfully at the end, never write on them in front of the giver, and carry a card case rather than fishing cards from a wallet.

If you don't have cards, apologize briefly and offer your details another way. It's a missed beat, not an offense.

![Two businesspeople exchanging business cards with both hands across a table](/images/blogs/japanese-business-etiquette-first-meeting/inline-1.webp)
*Both hands, print facing the recipient, and a genuine pause to read — the meishi exchange is thirty seconds that sets the tone.*

## Bowing, briefly

You'll be bowed to; a modest bow of your own — a natural inclination from the waist, hands at your sides — is a fine response. Depth and duration carry meaning between Japanese colleagues, but visitors aren't graded on the details. If a handshake is offered instead, take it. Many first meetings today involve a light hybrid of both, and that's perfectly normal.

## Where to sit

Japanese meeting rooms have a quiet geography. The seat furthest from the door — the kamiza — is the honored position, and as the guest it will be offered to you. The simple rule that keeps you right in every situation: wait to be directed to a seat rather than choosing one. The same logic extends to taxis and elevators, but in a meeting room, waiting one extra beat is all it takes.

## The rhythm of the conversation

First meetings in Japan often move slower than Western visitors expect. Small talk about your journey or the season isn't filler — it's the meeting establishing comfort. Decisions rarely happen in the room; a first meeting that ends with "we will consider this internally" has usually gone well, not badly. Pushing for an on-the-spot commitment works against you.

Silence is also part of the rhythm. A pause after you speak usually means consideration, not disagreement. Resist the urge to fill it.

![A meeting room with a low table, green tea served in cups, and notebooks laid out](/images/blogs/japanese-business-etiquette-first-meeting/inline-2.webp)
*Tea will usually arrive without being ordered. Take a sip after your hosts do — that's the entire rule.*

## Afterward

Send a brief thank-you email the same day or the next morning — short, warm, and referencing something concrete from the discussion. If a next step was mentioned, restate it. Consistent, prompt follow-up is where trust actually accumulates in Japanese business relationships; the meeting opens the door, the follow-through walks through it.

## What you're allowed to get wrong

Nearly everything else. Using the wrong bow depth, fumbling a phrase, forgetting which hand does what — none of it damages a relationship when your effort is visible. The etiquette that matters is the etiquette of intent: arriving early, treating the meishi with respect, listening more than pitching, and following up when you said you would. Do those four things and you'll be a better-mannered guest than you fear.
